## An incident, first

**A backfill overwrote three Supabase rows with data from three unrelated
matches.** `replay_log mirror --also 1 2 3` was run to backfill the newly
mirrored result columns. Supabase ids 1/2/3 are live-worker rows for CPL 2026
matches; the local corpus rows at those ids are 2017 Pakistan-in-Australia
ODIs. Migration `20260919000001` records that the two id spaces diverge and
that those three rows were deliberately not renumbered. The ids were passed
anyway.

| Severity | Columns | Recovered? |
|---|---|---|
| Overwrote real values with wrong ones | `competition`, `format`, `start_time` | Yes, from an in-session snapshot |
| Wrote wrong values into NULLs | `winner`, `target_runs`, `result_method`, `toss_winner`, `toss_decision` | Yes — all 107 rows were NULL, so NULL is provable |
| **Overwrote real values with wrong ones** | `team_a`, `team_b`, `venue_id` | **No** — now NULL |

`status`, `external_ids`, `match_id` and the `predictions` table were
untouched. Excluding `status` from the refresh was a deliberate design
decision made before the incident, and it held.

**Nothing read the damaged rows.** The daily calibration monitor last ran
2026-09-23T08:28Z, about eighteen hours before the damage and several hours
before the next run; match 3's 25 predictions are all `source='live'` and the
live population is `n=0, n_matches=0`, so the damaged competition and date
could not reach a report; the deployed landing page lists the ten most recent
matches, and the damage pushed these rows back to 2017, so they dropped *out*
of that list rather than appearing wrongly in it.

**It was found by chance, not by a check, and that is the important part.**
The tool printed `mirrored 106 of 107 requested match rows` — a success
message. The obvious verification, "are the result columns populated now?",
would have returned yes. It was caught only because the id-space warning was
fresh from an unrelated piece of reading.

### What changed as a result

Three guards, none of which existed:

1. **The id-space guard.** `mirror_match_rows` refuses any id whose Supabase
   row carries `external_ids->>'cricketdata'` — the mark of a live-worker row
   — and names the offending ids. The exact command that caused the damage now
   fails loudly.
2. **A dry run.** `mirror --dry-run` reports which rows would change and in
   which columns, and writes nothing.
3. **A change-set assertion.** `mirror --expect-changed N` refuses unless
   exactly N rows would change. Three rows behaving differently from the other
   104 is precisely a change set of an unexpected size.

Six tests cover them, including one that reproduces the incident.

### The finding under the finding: there is no backup

Grepping the repository for `pg_dump`/`pg_restore` returns only third-party
hits inside `.venv`. **No project-level backup mechanism exists**, and the
Supabase free tier provides no scheduled backups (daily backups begin at Pro;
PITR is a paid add-on). The only snapshot that existed during the incident was
a census JSON fetched twenty minutes earlier for an unrelated reason, covering
seven of the ten damaged columns. That is luck, not a recovery plan.

Most of what Supabase holds is *reproducible*: the 12,081 backfilled
predictions can be regenerated by `replay_log run` from the corpus and the
pinned artifact, and their outcomes rebuilt from `match_states`. **The 40 live
predictions are not.** They were made before the result existed, which is the
entire reason the live population is the honest column on `/accuracy`. A
replay produces `source='backfill'` rows by construction. Those 40 rows are
the only irreplaceable data in the system and nothing backs them up.

Carried forward as open item 1.

## `matches.status` follows the match

Nothing in the repository ran `UPDATE matches`. `_ensure_match_row`
early-returns an existing row and then `ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING`, and the loop
read match-end from an in-memory snapshot without writing it back.

Three changes. `_status_of` is now one derivation shared by the INSERT and by
`get_match_state` — they were previously two expressions that disagreed, the
insert being two-valued and the state three-valued, so a match first polled
before its toss went in as `'live'` and stayed there. `record_status` writes
transitions with an `IS DISTINCT FROM` guard, so the unchanged case touches no
row. And the status read moved **out** of `if predictor is not None and
deliveries:`.

That last one is the real bug. Proven by reverting it: two tests fail, and one
of them reports `match_end logged 0 times, expected once`. **A match that ended
on a poll returning no new deliveries never transitioned and never finished —
and the last poll of a match is the one most likely to be empty.**

`finish()` also became idempotent. A completed match stays in the provider's
live list for a while, so it was reached on every poll; standing rule 14 says
a line that repeats becomes furniture.

## The column census

`docs/column-census.md`, 172 columns across both databases, generated by
`web/scripts/make-column-census.mjs`.

**It found a fourth instance on its first run, and that is the argument for
it existing.** `matches.target_overs`: 98.2% populated locally, 0% on
Supabase. Three people had already looked directly at this table while
chasing the same bug in `winner`, `target_runs` and `result_method` — the
column was one line away in the same `\d matches` output every time — and
none of them saw it, because a schema listing renders a populated column and
an empty one identically. A tool that took forty minutes to write found in
one run what three careful readings of the same screen had missed. That is
not a comment on the readers; it is what makes this class of bug worth
automating rather than remembering. Adding it to the mirror and re-running the
backfill — through the new dry-run then `--expect-changed 100` workflow —
brought the census to **0 divergences**.

Worth noting what it correctly does *not* flag: `players.batting_hand` and
`teams.short_name` are empty on **both** databases. They are not divergences,
they are genuine absences Cricsheet cannot supply, and treating them as the
same problem would be wrong.

### The result line, and a bug in it

Derived from the mirrored `winner` plus the final payload and
`prediction_outcomes.actual.batting_team_won`. **The margin is deliberately
not computed**: the final payload is the state *before* the last ball, so
"won by 4 wickets" is not derivable while "chase needed 1 off 1" is exactly
true.

The first version read `Finland won · 74 needed off 17`, which says Finland
needed 74. Checked against the corpus: match 4465, Finland won, **Sweden** was
chasing. The requirement belongs to the side batting second, which is not
always the winner. Now `chase needed 74 off 17`, and where the team is unknown
the subject becomes the target — `Target defended`, not the nonsense `Chase
defended`.

# Follow-up, same session

Three things from the open list, closed before moving on.

## 1. Supabase is backed up, and the restore is verified

`.github/workflows/backup.yml`, nightly at 04:00 UTC — an hour after the
calibration monitor, so a dump is never taken mid-write of a
`calibration_runs` row.

`pg_dump` through the session pooler (§2.4 — session mode; the transaction
pooler lacks the session state pg_dump needs and fails partway rather than
refusing up front), restored into a throwaway `postgres:17.6`, then compared
against the source. Kept as an Action artifact for 14 days, **not** committed:
a nightly blob in git history is unremovable, and a database dump in a
repository is a credential leak waiting for one careless column.

**The whole schema, not a curated list.** A hand-maintained list of "the
tables that matter" is the same failure mode the census exists to catch — an
enumeration that goes stale the moment a migration adds a table, and reports
success while omitting it. The dump is 7.5MB; being clever was not worth it.

**A backup nobody has restored is not a backup**, so the job restores and
verifies rather than checking the file exists. Per table, on both sides: row
count and a content digest that hashes each row and aggregates ordered by that
hash, so it is independent of physical row order. Counts alone would pass a
restore that produced the right number of wrong rows.

Run end to end locally before committing it: **21 tables, 99,159 rows, dumped,
restored and matching row for row.** Two things that only showed up by running
it — the dump emits `CREATE SCHEMA public`, which collides with the one every
new database already has, so the restore needs a fresh database with its
public schema dropped; and the RLS policies name `anon` and `authenticated`,
which a plain Postgres does not have. The roles are **derived from the dump**
rather than hardcoded, so a migration adding a policy for a new role does not
break this job months later at 04:00 for a reason nobody would guess.

Twelve tests cover the refusal paths, all on ways the job could report success
while the backup is useless: missing file, zero bytes, an error message
written to stdout, a **truncated** dump (large, syntactically valid, missing
the completion marker — the one that would otherwise get through), a short
restore, a same-count-different-content restore, and an empty source table.

## 2. The `innings` disagreement — the readers were wrong, not the writers

A correction to this session's own write-up above, which said "the HTTP
service writes `innings` as NULL". **That was wrong.** `serving/app.py` writes
the full ball key and carries the same `ON CONFLICT ... DO NOTHING` as the
other two writers. All three first-party writers are identical on this point.

What actually happened: the key is optional on the request, and
`drive_replay.load_balls()` never sent `over_num`/`ball_in_over`. The endpoint
did exactly what it was asked. **The NULL came from the caller.**

The reader picture, traced exhaustively: `calibration_monitor`,
`resolve_outcomes`, `replay_log`'s three gates, `/matches`, `/api/live` and
`make-strip-fixture` all filter on `innings IS NOT NULL`. The **match page**
did not — not in its server loader, not in `reconcile()`, and not in the
Realtime handler. Migration `20260918000003` states the contract in its own
comment: *"every Phase 3 reader filters on innings IS NOT NULL"*. So the match
page was the surface that was wrong, and it was the only user-facing one
rendering unkeyed rows — drawing match 13143's curve out of ten interleaved
runs of a twelve-ball deploy smoke test while `/matches` listed the same match
as never replayed.

Fixed in all three places. Realtime cannot express it — PostgREST takes one
filter and it is spent on `match_id` — so that one drops the row in the
handler. Two tests cover it, and the first version of the streaming test
**passed with the filter removed**, because the probe row used `p: 0.5`, the
same value as the prediction already on screen. A test whose fixture cannot be
distinguished from the correct answer proves nothing.

Found in passing: `agent_tools/routes.py`'s `get_live_prediction` selects
`win_probability`, which is not a column and never has been — the table is
`payload JSONB`. That endpoint has raised `UndefinedColumn` on every call it
has ever received. No test exercises it against a database, which is how it
survived. Now `(payload->>'p')::float`, with the innings and `prediction_type`
filters it was also missing.

## 3. The 108 duplicates — what made them, and can it still happen

**One line: `drive_replay` posted balls without the ball key, so `app.py`'s
`ON CONFLICT` target was unsatisfiable — NULLs never conflict — and the
transport's retried POST wrote each ball a second time.**

Yes, it could still happen, on every `drive_replay` run. Closed by making
`load_balls()` select and send `d.over_num, d.ball_in_over`, which also
required changing its `ORDER BY ms.balls_bowled` to `ORDER BY d.over_num,
d.ball_in_over` — an extra does not advance `balls_bowled`, so an over
containing a wide came back with two rows sharing a sort value and posted in
arbitrary order. `replay_log.py` orders the same way for the same reason.

That was the last first-party producer of unkeyed rows. The two remaining are
`web/scripts/check-anon-access.mjs` (which deletes its own probe row) and
`web/scripts/measure-realtime-coldstart.mjs` (which does not) — both
diagnostics, neither on a serving path.

## Standing rules

Rules 1–8 are in `docs/phase2-closeout.md`, 9 and 10 in
`docs/phase3-closeout.md`, 11–14 in `docs/phase6-session1.md`, 15 in
`docs/ui-session1.md`. Sixteen and seventeen are below.

**16. A change that MUTATES existing rows is a different risk class from one
that only inserts, and needs a dry run and an expected-change-count assertion
BEFORE it runs — not after it goes wrong.**

`mirror_match_rows` grew five columns and its `ON CONFLICT` went from
`DO NOTHING` to `DO UPDATE SET`, so that 107 rows written before those columns
existed could receive them. That is the correct design and it is still the
design. What was missing is that the same edit turned a statement which could
only ever add rows into one that could silently rewrite them, and nothing —
not the plan, not the function, not the CLI, not the reviewer — marked the
moment that changed.

`--also 1 2 3` then wrote three matches' data onto three unrelated matches and
printed `mirrored 106 of 107 requested match rows`.

**The asymmetry is the whole rule. A wrong INSERT is visible as a new row: it
has an id nobody expected, it appears in a count, and the thing it duplicates
is still there. A wrong UPDATE leaves a well-formed row describing something
else, and the thing it replaced is gone.** The first is found by looking; the
second is found by having kept a copy. Every check that existed at the time
measured the first kind — rows touched, rows inserted, no errors raised — and
none of them could see the second.

The two mechanisms this demands are cheap and were written in under an hour
afterwards. `--dry-run` reports which rows would change and in which columns.
`--expect-changed N` refuses unless the change set is exactly the size the
caller predicted, because **a change set of an unexpected size means the two
sides disagree about something nobody has looked at** — three rows behaving
differently from the other 104 is precisely that shape, and the assertion
would have stopped it without anyone having to remember the id-space problem.

Scope limit: this is not "add a dry run to everything". An idempotent insert,
a create-if-absent, an append to a log — none of these need it, and demanding
ceremony for them is how the ceremony gets skipped when it matters. The
trigger is narrow and checkable: **does this statement have an UPDATE path
over rows that already exist?** If yes, it needs both before it runs once.

Closed for this instance by `--dry-run`, `--expect-changed`, and the id-space
guard that refuses the specific ids involved. Also by the backup above, which
is the answer to the version of this that gets through anyway.

**17. A check can be correctly built, correctly wired, and still never
execute, because its TRIGGER never fired. "Is it running" and "is it
passing" are different questions, and only the second one is ever asked.**

`web/lib/types.ts` was found missing two tables that migrations had added
four days earlier. CI has a job for exactly this. It has no `if:`, no path
filter, and it is its own job specifically because rule 8's instance taught
that a sibling failure can mask a step. It was built correctly and it did
not fire.

`origin/main` was **25 commits behind local**, and the last CI run of any
kind was green, four days old, against a commit none of that work was on. A
job triggered by `push` is blind to everything unpushed, however well it is
written. The Python suite, the web suite, both generated-file diffs and the
eval staleness gate were all equally blind, for the same reason, over the
same window — four sessions of work whose only validation was local.

**This is distinct from 8 and 14 and it is worth keeping distinct.** Rule 8
is a check that ran and was masked: it executed, its result was discarded.
Rule 14 is a check that spoke and was ignored: it executed, its result was
read past. **This is a check that never executed at all, and therefore
produced no result to mask or ignore.** The three feel similar from the
outside — in every case a real defect survived a gate built to catch it —
and they have different fixes. Masking is fixed by isolating the job.
Tolerating is fixed by removing the noise. Never firing can only be fixed by
moving the gate to a moment that cannot be skipped, because no amount of
improvement to a gate makes it run.

The tell is that **a green history is evidence of nothing without a date and
a commit.** "CI is passing" was true and meaningless: it was passing on
`1503062`, which predated every line of the work in question. Any claim that
a gate is protecting something needs to name what it last ran against.

The generalisation: **for every gate, ask what invokes it, and whether that
invocation is guaranteed by the workflow it protects.** A gate on `push`
protects pushed work. A gate in CI protects what reaches CI. A gate nobody
can commit without — a hook, a pre-commit, a step inside the one command
people actually run — protects the work. That is a strictly smaller set of
gates than "all the checks we have", and it is the only set that is
load-bearing for work in progress.

Scope limit: this is not an argument for moving everything into a hook. The
Python suite is nine to thirteen minutes and belongs exactly where it is;
a hook that slow gets disabled within a week, which is a worse outcome than
CI-only. The rule is to know which gates are covering in-progress work and
which are only covering what has already left the machine, and to be honest
about the difference rather than reading "CI is green" as though it answered
both.

Closed by `.githooks/pre-push`, which runs the staleness gates and the two
cheap web gates at the last moment before work leaves the machine, skips
loudly rather than blocking when offline, and states in its own header that
the Python suite is not covered. Enabled per clone with
`git config core.hooksPath .githooks`.

And closed, for this instance, by pushing: the 25 commits reached CI for the
first time on 2026-09-24. Two jobs went red. The `types` failure was
predicted before the run and confirmed the diagnosis. The other was not.

# Session 4 — players

## What is live

`/players` (8,575 with a record) and `/player/[playerId]`, plus two derived
tables built locally and synced with a verified content hash.

161 web tests, 263 api tests.

## The data had to be built, not queried

§4.4 wants career aggregates and splits by phase and format. All of it is
computed from `deliveries`, which is empty on Supabase — so the page cannot
compute it at request time, and §7 listed the source as an open decision.

Measured before choosing: **9,615** rows per (player, format), **21,451** with
phase. Comparable to `venue_asof_summary` (10,508) and `elo_asof_summary`
(25,290), both of which already cross by exactly this route. So:
`player_career_summary` (37,346 rows) and `player_index` (8,575), built by
`features.player_summary`, synced by `sync_reference_tables` with the same
replace-and-verify.

## More than half the corpus's players have no record

**18,468 named, 8,080 have ever batted, 6,054 have ever bowled, 8,575 have
done either.** The remaining ~9,900 appear because a team sheet named them.
An index listing all 18,468 would be the problem `/matches` already had to
solve — a page that looks like an index and is mostly rows going nowhere. So
`player_index` holds only players with a record, and the heading says which
number is which. `/player/1` correctly 404s: that player has none.

## Search reuses the resolver, by carrying its output

§4.4's acceptance is that "Kohli" and "Virat Kohli" reach the same player,
and the instruction is to reuse the resolver rather than write a second
matcher. The resolver is Python and the search box is TypeScript, so what
crosses is its **output**: `normalize_name` and `surname_key` run during the
rebuild and are stored on `player_index`. There is one implementation of what
a name means and the web layer does not own it.

This matters more than it sounds. The corpus spells the player **"V Kohli"**,
so "virat kohli" is not a prefix, not a substring and not a word of the
stored name — it can only be reached through the surname key. A naive
`includes` passes the first query and fails the second.

Three Kohlis exist, so results rank by career runs within a tier: someone
typing the surname means the one with 28,134 runs, not the one with 59.

## Deviations from §4.4, both measured

**Not virtualised.** §4.4 says the table must be, assuming 18,468 rows. The
index is 8,575 and the table never renders more than 50 — the top by runs
until someone types, then the matches. A windowing dependency for fifty rows
is what CLAUDE.md §2 rules out.

**No by-venue table or recent innings.** Both are in the corpus and neither
is in the summary. They are a rebuild away rather than a model away, and the
page says so under "Not built yet" rather than omitting them silently.

## The cricket rules, stated because SQL cannot show them

A reviewer can check the query; they cannot check whether it encodes the game
correctly. So `features/player_summary.py` states each rule it implements: a
batter faces every delivery except a wide (a no-ball **is** faced); a batter's
runs are `runs_batter` only; a `retired *` type ends an innings without being
a dismissal, and counting it would deflate the most-quoted number on the page;
a bowler bowls legal deliveries only; byes and leg-byes are **not** charged to
the bowler; and only bowled, caught, caught and bowled, lbw, stumped and hit
wicket are the bowler's wicket — a run out is not.

Checked against two known players rather than trusted: **V Kohli T20, 390
innings, avg 43.0, SR 135.2, death-overs SR 189.2**; **JJ Bumrah T20, economy
7.06 overall rising to 7.81 at the death**. Both the right shape. The phase
splits fall six balls short of the career line for Kohli — those are super-over
deliveries, which have no phase and are deliberately in the total and out of
the splits.

## The refusal is the page

Where a form curve would go, `/player/[id]` says the system records what a
player has done and does not estimate what they can do now, and that the two
are different questions. Same refusal `get_player_form` makes in the agent.
§4.4 calls it the clearest demonstration of the product's stance a visitor

Every rate carries its sample size, and a line below 20 innings renders
hollow — an outlined figure with its innings count in the title, not a lighter
grey, because a lighter grey still reads as a number you can use. Verified on
a real player: 16 hollow cells, each captioned "4 innings — below the
20-innings threshold".

table says "never dismissed in N innings" instead. `batting_hand`,

with one line saying they are absent in the source rather than missing for
this player.
